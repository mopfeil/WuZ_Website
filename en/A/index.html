The Hole in the Floor

A flat plate printed in four colours and a sight with a small peephole. Look through it and you see a deep hole with a staircase and an open trapdoor. Stand next to it and you see only a strangely distorted pattern.

What this is about

A magic trick bursts a firm expectation. At exactly that moment we feel wonder, and wonder makes us curious and open: we want to know how it can be, and we are ready to correct our picture of the world. Every science starts with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment. First play and wonder, then make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. More under Background.

Before you start: There is no secret to keep here. The anamorphosis is an optical illusion, and the explanation is part of the piece. But let your audience look and wonder first, before you explain.

What your audience experiences

A plate lies on the table, with a thin post standing in front of it. The post has a hole in it. Your spectator closes one eye and looks through the hole with the other. A shaft opens up in the floor. A wooden staircase leads down along the left wall, and at the far edge a trapdoor stands upright and casts its shadow on the floor.

Then they take a step to the side. The hole is gone. On the table lies a flat plate with a skewed, stretched drawing that looks like nothing at all.

What you need

  • The plate (120 × 162 × 3 mm) in four colours: a light floor, black for shadows and outlines, grey for the lit walls and the trapdoor's cast shadow, wood brown for the steps and the trapdoor. In the magic case it lies in the lid under the NIM board. The push holes in the bottom of the lid lift both out.
  • The plug-in sight in three parts, in the lower level of the case under the insert:
    • the arm, with a dovetail tongue at one end and a socket at the other,
    • the lower and the upper post (8 × 8 mm each). The peephole (3 mm across) is near the top of the upper post.
  • Two short pieces of filament (1.75 mm) as pins for the post joint. Spares are in the FILAMENT compartment of the case.
  • If you like, a phone to photograph the view or show it on a screen.

How the prop works

The picture on the plate is computed. First a three-dimensional scene was described: a hole 60 × 70 mm and 30 mm deep, five steps along the left wall, a trapdoor 40 mm high at the far edge, light from the front left and above. Then every point of this scene was followed along a straight line towards a fixed eye point, and drawn on the plate where that line meets the plate. For this one eye, every drawn point therefore lies exactly where the real point of the scene would be seen.

The peephole sits exactly at this eye point: 180 mm above the table, 160 mm in front of the front edge of the plate and 20 mm to the right of its centre. The arm and the post keep it there.

Side view, to scale. Dashed is what the eye believes it sees. The top of the trapdoor is drawn on the plate where the line of sight meets the plate (dot on the right), far behind the hole.

That is why the plate looks so odd from above: the trapdoor is stretched into a long trapezoid that covers almost half the plate, its round handle is a tall oval, and the staircase gets narrower towards the front.

Preparation

  1. Join the posts. Lay the lower and the upper post on top of each other at the lap joint (14 mm) so that the holes line up. Push a piece of filament through each pair of holes. Trim the pins until they no longer stick out.
  2. Plug in the post. Push the lower post into the socket of the arm. The peephole points towards the plate, along the arm.
  3. Slide the sight onto the plate. The plate lies flat on the table, with the narrow side with the dovetail cut-out towards you. Slide the tongue of the arm into this cut-out from the front. The post now stands 160 mm in front of the plate.
  4. Check it yourself. Look through the peephole with one eye, close up. If the hole opens up, everything is set up correctly. Make sure the table is level and the tongue is pushed all the way in.

Performance

  1. Lead them in. Have your spectator come to the table without looking down at the plate first. Set it up so that they approach from the side of the sight.
  2. Look through. Ask them to close one eye and bring the other close to the peephole. Ask what they see and how deep they think the hole is.
  3. Explore the space. Let them describe it: Where does the staircase lead? Where does the light come from? Which way does the trapdoor's shadow fall?
  4. The step to the side. Now they take their eye away from the hole and look at the plate from above or from the side with both eyes. The hole has vanished and the plate is flat.
  5. Back and forth. Let them switch several times. Many people want to see whether the hole is there again the second time. It is, every time.
  6. The explanation. Explain how the picture was made (see above), and show what the trapdoor and the stairs really look like on the plate.

Why it works

The image on the retina is flat. Whether something is deep, near or far has to be inferred by the brain from cues in the image: from vanishing lines, from one thing hiding another, from light and shadow. In everyday life these cues are very reliable, so the brain trusts them. The plate supplies exactly these cues, but only for the one point where the peephole sits.

The shadows do a lot of the work. The left wall and the side of the stairs lie in the dark, the rim casts a shadow into the hole, and the trapdoor casts its shadow on the floor next to it. All of this fits a single light source at the front left and above, which is exactly what the brain expects from a real pit.

The small hole also forces you to look with one eye. With two eyes the brain would get two slightly different images, and from them it could tell that the plate is flat. With one eye this cue is missing. One step to the side and the drawing no longer fits: it falls apart into a stretched pattern.

Tips

  • The illusion is strongest if your spectator has not seen the plate from above beforehand. So put it down just before they look through.
  • For a larger group: hold the phone camera right behind the peephole and show the picture on a screen. Then swing the camera to the side, and everyone sees the hole fall apart.
  • Even, not too bright light is better than a strong spotlight. A hard shadow on the plate that does not match the drawn lighting spoils the illusion.
  • If the post wobbles, the filament pins are too loose. Take a fresh piece from the case.
  • To pack up: pull out the tongue and pull the post out of the socket. You can leave the two post halves joined if they fit the compartment, otherwise push the pins out.

And what does this have to do with science?

Your spectator saw a hole that does not exist, and kept seeing it even after they knew the plate was flat. Depth is not in the plate. It is something our brain infers from cues, and it does so so quickly and so confidently that it feels like seeing, not like reasoning.

That is exactly why the scientific method distrusts first impressions. An impression can be completely convincing and still be right from only one point of view. The step to the side is what a second look with a different method is in research: what is really there holds up from another side too.

If you like, you can draw a picture like this yourself. The book describes an exercise with paper, pencil and a phone camera. Placing the cues yourself shows you how seeing works.
