The Board of Wonders

Nine squares. On eight of them lies something we use to know the world, on the ninth a question mark. Your spectator chooses freely where to start and moves however they like. In the end their coin stands on the question mark anyway. With the story as audio.

What this is about

Knowledge has never been as easy to get as it is today, and yet distrust of science keeps growing. More facts alone do little about it. The legal scholar Dan Kahan showed that people with more scientific knowledge are often even further apart on contested issues: they use what they know to defend the views of their own group.

What is missing is wonder. We feel wonder when the world turns out differently from what we expected. The brain predicted something, and something else happened. In that moment it does not defend itself, it learns. The philosopher Helen De Cruz therefore describes wonder as a feeling that tells us: your picture of the world needs revising.

Magic creates this moment reliably and with little effort. Anyone who sees a trick immediately asks: how can that be? Every science begins with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment along the triad of playing, wondering, understanding: try something out, be surprised, make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. The idea behind it is that wonder helps us learn better, opens our thinking and makes us less likely to mistake a mere guess for a fact. More under Background and in the book “Wissenschaft und Zauberkunst” (Science and Magic).

Before you start: The moves and what gets taken away are not printed anywhere on the board. You call them out. Learn the sequence below by heart or keep a small note that only you can see. To get started, the story is also available as audio.

What your audience experiences

Nine tiles lie on the board: 1 lens, 2 proof, 3 hourglass, 4 deck of cards, 5 candle, 6 question mark, 7 book, 8 compass, 9 key. Eight things with which we come to know the world, and one question. Your spectator puts the coin on any square and moves freely, move by move. After every move one or two tiles are taken away, and the coin is never standing on them.

In the end only one tile is left: the question mark, and that is exactly where the coin stands. Its underside reads: “Nur die Frage bleibt” (only the question remains).

What you need

  • The base plate (85 × 85 mm) with the grid of nine pockets. Under every pocket there is a push hole.
  • The nine tiles with number and picture. Press the bottom left corner and the tile tips up so you can take it. Tile 6 has the question mark on top and the sentence “Nur die Frage bleibt.” underneath.
  • The coin as playing piece.
  • The frame, only for the performance with a spectator (version 2). It goes around the grid and is labelled on both sides: on the front letters A B C on the left and numbers 1 2 3 on top, on the back the other way round.
  • For storage the sleeve, a rubber band and the QR plate. The assembled board fits exactly into the sleeve. The band runs lengthwise through the notches at both ends. Press the coin into the hole of the QR plate; the band runs through the slot of the coin. That holds everything together.

The rules for your spectator

  • A step goes to a neighbouring square: up, down, left or right, never diagonally.
  • Going back and forth and in circles is allowed. The spectator decides the route.
  • Empty squares may be crossed, but no move may end on an empty square.

Version 1: The story on your own

This is how you get to know the trick, and how you can pass it on: someone plays along alone while the story is told. You do not need the frame for this. Put the tiles into the grid in the order 1 to 9, row by row from the top left, and listen:

The story comes from the booklet that accompanies the book: a student asks her professor whether everything is predetermined, and the professor lets her try it out. Here it is adapted to the tiles of the board. This is the sequence:

  1. “Place the coin on any tile you like.” Every one is fine.
  2. “Move as many steps as the number on your tile shows.” The book (7) goes: what is written in it, somebody else has already thought.
  3. “Move five steps.” The deck of cards (4) goes: you do not want to rely on luck and chance.
  4. “Move two steps.” The compass (8) goes: you do not need a direction someone else gives you.
  5. “Move three steps.” The lens (1) and the key (9) go: looking more closely helps little if you do not know what you are looking for, and a key only opens doors somebody has already built.
  6. “Move three steps once more.” The proof (2) goes: a proof ends a question.
  7. “Make one last step.” The hourglass (3) and the candle (5) go: time has run out, the light has gone.
  8. The reveal. The coin stands on the question mark. Turn the tile over: “Everything known has fallen away. Only the question remains.”

Version 2: Your spectator lays out the tiles

Now your spectator lays out the tiles, any way they like. Since nobody then knows where each thing lies, you no longer name things when taking tiles away, but squares: C1, B1 and so on. That is what the frame is for.

  1. Laying out. Give your spectator the eight tiles without the question mark. They put them into any pockets. One pocket stays empty. You keep the question mark.
  2. The trial move. “Put the coin on a tile and move three steps, so I can see that you know the rules.” The move must not end on the empty pocket. If they started on a square in the middle of a side (top, bottom, left or right middle), they put the coin back on the starting square afterwards. If they started in a corner or in the centre, the coin stays where it arrived.
  3. Placing the question mark. The coin now always stands on one of the four squares in the middle of a side. You place the question mark on the square in the middle of the right side. If the coin stands exactly there, place it on the square in the middle of the bottom side instead. If a tile already lies on the target square, it goes into the empty pocket.
  4. Putting on the frame. If the question mark lies on the right, the letters go on the left. If it lies at the bottom, turn the frame over; then the numbers are on the left. Either way the question mark is on B3.
  5. The first square. Take away the tile on C1.
  6. The moves. Now you call out the moves as in version 1, only with squares instead of things:
    • “Move five steps.” B1 goes.
    • “Move two steps.” C2 goes.
    • “Move three steps.” A1 and C3 go.
    • “Move three steps once more.” A2 goes.
    • “Make one last step.” A3 and B2 go.
  7. The reveal. The coin stands on B3, the question mark. Turn it over: “Only the question remains.”

Why it works

Think of the board as a chessboard. The corners and the centre are light, the four squares in the middle of each side are dark (grey in the drawing). Every step changes the colour. An odd number of steps lands on the other colour, an even number on the same one.

Version 1: The first move is the trick. The light squares carry the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, the dark ones the even numbers. From a light square you therefore walk an odd number of steps and land on dark; from a dark square an even number and stay on dark. After the first move the coin always stands on a dark square.

Version 2: Here the trial move does the job. Three steps from a corner or the centre always lead to a dark square. Anyone who started on a dark square goes back to it.

From then on you know for every move which colour the coin lands on, and you only take away tiles of the other colour:

  • The coin is on dark; the light square bottom left goes (book, C1).
  • After 5 steps light; the dark square in the middle of the left side goes (deck of cards, B1).
  • After 2 steps light; the dark square in the middle of the bottom goes (compass, C2).
  • After 3 steps dark; the light corners top left and bottom right go (lens and key, A1 and C3).
  • After 3 steps light; the dark square in the middle of the top goes (proof, A2). Only two light squares are left: top right and the centre.
  • After 1 step dark. The only dark square with anything left on it is the question mark.

And why may you turn the frame over? Turned over, it mirrors the board along the diagonal from top left to bottom right. Rows become columns, the middle of the right side becomes the middle of the bottom side, and the colours stay as they are. That is why the calls C1, B1 and so on hit exactly the right squares in both positions. We worked through all starting squares and all permitted routes in both versions and both positions of the frame: every route ends on the question mark.

Tips

  • Have your spectator count out loud. Then you see every step and nobody miscounts unnoticed. A miscounted move is the only thing that can spoil the trick.
  • If the coin stands on a square that is about to be taken away, someone miscounted. Have the move repeated.
  • The tiles come out easily: press the bottom left corner and the opposite corner lifts.
  • You can also announce the removal in advance: “After that I will take away C1.” That is bolder, because you predict where the coin will certainly not be.
  • The QR plate on the sleeve leads to this page. Whoever gets the board from you finds the story to listen to here.

And what does this have to do with science?

Your spectator had a choice at every move, and yet the result was fixed from the start. The freedom was real; it just concerned something that did not matter for the end. What mattered was a hidden rule: the colour of the square.

Science works the same way. Behind many seemingly random processes there is a quantity that does not change, an invariant. Whoever finds it can predict without knowing every single step. And whoever knows that such hidden rules exist looks more closely at the next “completely free” result.
