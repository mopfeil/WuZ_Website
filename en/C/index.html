The Baffling Boxes

Three identical little boxes, stacked on top of each other. Your spectator lifts all three together, then only the top one. And the single box feels heavier than all three together.

What this is about

A magic trick bursts a firm expectation. At exactly that moment we feel wonder, and wonder makes us curious and open: we want to know how it can be, and we are ready to correct our picture of the world. Every science starts with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment. First play and wonder, then make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. More under Background.

Before you read on: there is a small secret here, namely which box is filled. Keep it to yourself while your spectator is wondering. The special thing about this trick, though, is that the illusion stays even when they know the secret. So you may reveal it at the end.

What your audience sees

On the table stands a small stack of three boxes that look exactly alike. You lift the whole stack, put it down and then lift only the top box. Your spectator copies you.

The single box feels clearly heavier than all three together. That cannot be, because it is part of the stack. Even so, that is how it feels, every single time.

What you need

  • Three identical boxes with lids (model “flat3”). One box measures 37.2 × 23.2 mm on the outside, 39.5 × 25.5 × 9.7 mm with the lid on. Inside there is room for three weights of 11 × 19 × 3.7 mm each, lying side by side with their long edges touching.
  • Three such weights. They go into only one of the three boxes. The other two stay empty.
  • Some glue to fix the weights in place.

In the magic case the three boxes lie stacked in their compartment.

How the boxes work

All three boxes are built the same and look the same from outside. The lid fits over the box like a cap. On the two long sides there are springy tabs with small barbs that click into grooves in the outer wall. On the printed lid the barb is flat: once clicked shut, the lid does not come off again. There is also a second lid version with a sloped barb that can be pulled off with some force.

The bottom of each box sticks out 1.5 mm below the rim of its lid and drops into an equally deep recess in the lid underneath. So the three boxes sit neatly on top of each other and do not slide. The stack is almost 26 mm high.

Walls, bottom and lid are deliberately thin. This keeps the empty boxes light and makes the difference to the filled one bigger.

Preparation

Lay the three weights side by side in one of the boxes and glue them in. Only when the glue is dry do you click the lid on. Close the other two boxes empty. For the performance, stack the three boxes with the filled one on top.

Performance

  1. Show. Put the stack on the table. Three boxes, all alike.
  2. Demonstrate. Hold the bottom box by its sides and lift the whole stack a little. Put it down. Then lift only the top box and put it back on the stack.
  3. Let them try. Your spectator first lifts all three together and feels how heavy the stack is.
  4. The single box. Now they lift only the top one. It feels heavier than all three did together. Let them switch back and forth a few times.
  5. Think it over. Ask them how this can be. The top box was part of the stack, so the stack must weigh at least as much.
  6. Reveal, if you like. Tell them that the top box holds weights and the others are empty. Then have them lift again. The illusion stays.

What is behind it

When you lift something, your brain estimates beforehand how heavy it will be and sets the force accordingly. With the stack it sees three boxes and expects each one to weigh about a third. But most of the weight is in the top box. The expectation is let down, you need more force than you thought, and this difference is felt as “heavier”.

Psychologists call this the size-weight illusion or Charpentier illusion, after Augustin Charpentier, who described it in 1891. In the magic literature the trick is known as “The Baffling Blocks”. Flanagan and Beltzner showed in 2000 that the illusion persists even after many repetitions. Despite more than a hundred years of research, there is still no truly convincing explanation of why it happens.

Tips

  • One of the three boxes has a QR code on its underside that leads to these instructions. Put it at the bottom of the stack; the code then rests on the table and goes unnoticed.
  • Keep track of which box is the filled one and always put it on top. The boxes all look alike.
  • The boxes are small. Have the stack lifted by the bottom box, or only the upper ones come up.
  • Do not let anyone compare the boxes one by one while you want to keep the secret. They would notice at once that the lower ones are light.
  • The trick also works with three matchboxes, with two batteries in the top one and the others empty.

And what does this have to do with science?

Your spectator knows that the top box cannot be heavier than the stack it belongs to. And even once they know the secret, it still feels that way. Perception is shaped in advance by expectation, and knowledge alone does not switch that off. This is exactly why research does not rely on how things feel to the observers. You measure with scales instead of your hand, and you design experiments so that the expectations of those involved cannot colour the result, for example with blind trials.
