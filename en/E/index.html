Measure first, then calculate

The experiment with the rolling track and the six discs. Your participants measure the circumference of circles and ellipses, and only afterwards get a formula that is convincingly justified and still does not fit. Whom do they believe, the track or the formula?

What this is about

A magic trick bursts a firm expectation. At exactly that moment we feel wonder, and wonder makes us curious and open: we want to know how it can be, and we are ready to correct our picture of the world. Every science starts with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment. First play and wonder, then make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. More under Background.

Before you read on: there is no trick here, but there is a reveal. The formula for the circumference of an ellipse that your participants get is wrong on purpose. Do not give this away until they have decided how they explain the discrepancies. Otherwise the experiment is over before it begins.

What your audience sees

On the table there is a narrow track with a groove and a millimetre scale, and six flat discs: two circles, K1 and K2, and four ellipses, E1 to E4. Your participants measure the length and width of each disc. Then they stand the disc on its edge in the groove and roll it exactly once round. The distance it travels is its circumference.

Only when the table is full do they calculate. For the circles, the familiar formula fits to within one or two per cent. That shows the track measures accurately. For the ellipses there is a formula with a neat justification. It fits E1 and E2 as well. For E3 and E4 it is far off, and always too low. Now your participants have to decide: did they measure wrongly, did the disc slip, are the discs printed inaccurately, or is the formula wrong?

What you need

  • The rolling track. 195 mm long, 34 mm wide, 10 mm high. A groove 6.8 mm wide and 5 mm deep runs along the middle, with a scale from 0 to 170 mm beside it. The zero line is also marked on the floor of the groove.
  • Six discs, 6 mm thick. K1 is 30 mm across, K2 40 mm. The ellipses measure E1 50 × 40, E2 48 × 32, E3 64 × 16 and E4 72 × 12 mm. The discs carry only their label, no dimensions, because those are to be measured. Each disc has a red mark on its rim.
  • Two sheets for each participant. The first has a table with the columns disc, length A, width B and three columns for the circumference (1st, 2nd and 3rd roll). The second has the formulas for checking. Hand out the second sheet later, or print it on the back.
  • Pens and a calculator; a smartphone will do.

In the magic case the track lies in the MASSRILLE compartment of the lower level. The discs sit in two compartments of the insert, stacked as E1, E2, K2 and as E4, E3, K1.

How the prop works

A disc that makes exactly one turn without slipping travels exactly its circumference along the track. That holds for a circle and for an ellipse alike. So you need no tape measure that can slide off the edge. You put the disc on zero, roll it and read off the value.

The red mark sits at the end of the short axis. When the mark is at the bottom, the disc rests on its flat side and stands steadily at the start. After one full turn the mark is at the bottom again, and that is where you read the scale.

The flat ellipses E3 and E4 tip over their pointed ends as they roll, and their centre rises and falls a lot. The groove keeps them upright; you guide them with one finger on top of the rim. At the start, E3 and E4 stick out up to 16 mm beyond the left end of the track. That does no harm, because the disc only stands on its contact point in the groove.

Length A and width B are measured by laying the disc flat on the scale. A is the longest diameter, B the shortest.

Preparation

Lay the track flat on the table with the six discs next to it. Print the two sheets. Roll each disc a few times yourself beforehand, so that you can demonstrate the rolling and know how E3 and E4 behave. If a disc slips on the track, put a strip of masking tape in the groove.

Procedure

  1. Measure first. Hand out the first sheet. Your participants measure all six discs before they see any formula. They measure length and width by laying the disc flat on the scale.
  2. Demonstrate the rolling. Disc on its edge in the groove, red mark at the bottom on 0. Roll it on slowly and with light pressure for one turn, until the mark is at the bottom again, and read off there. With E3 and E4 a finger on top of the rim guides the disc, otherwise they tip over easily.
  3. Roll twice. Every disc is rolled twice. If the two values differ by more than 2 mm, roll a third time.
  4. Then calculate: the circles. Only when the table is full does the second sheet come out. For a circle, circumference = 3.14 · D. Your participants work out the deviation for K1 and K2: (measured − calculated) / calculated · 100 %. For the circles it fits to within 1 to 2 %. Under 2 % means: you roll like a pro. This confirms their own measuring accuracy.
  5. The ellipses. Now comes the formula with its justification: “An ellipse is a squashed circle with length A and width B. It has exactly as much area as a circle with diameter √(A · B). When it is squashed, it gets as much longer in one direction as it gets narrower in the other. So the circumference stays the same too: circumference = 3.14 · √(A · B).” E1 and E2 fit as well, with about 1 and 3 %. E3 and E4 do not.
  6. Do not give it away. Do not say what is right. Ask instead: “Whom do you believe, the track or the formula?” Anyone who wants to roll again may do so. Then everyone ticks how they explain the discrepancies for E3 and E4: I measured wrongly. The disc slipped while rolling. The discs are printed inaccurately. The formula is wrong.
  7. The quick test. Finally ask: how long must the rim be at least? While rolling, the disc has to go once along its whole length and back again. So the circumference of E4, with A = 72 mm, is more than 2 · 72 = 144 mm. The formula says 92 mm. That cannot be right.
  8. The reveal. The track is right, the formula is wrong.

What is behind it

Only the first part of the justification is correct: the ellipse really does have the same area as a circle with diameter √(A · B). But the same area does not mean the same circumference. The longer and narrower a shape is, the longer its edge for the same area. That is why the formula is right for circles, almost right for nearly round ellipses and far too low for elongated ones.

There is no simple exact formula for the circumference of an ellipse. The values in the table come from a very good approximation found by the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan in 1914:

circumference ≈ 1.571 · [ 3 (A + B) − √((3A + B) · (A + 3B)) ]

The discs are the paper templates from the lecture-hall experiment at a scale of 1:5. The ratio of A to B is the same, so the wrong formula is off by the same percentage as with the large templates and a tape measure. There, E3 measures 320 × 80 mm and has a circumference of 686 mm, while the formula says 502 mm.

Careful rolling should be accurate to about 1 to 2 mm. That is an estimate; it has not been tried out yet. For E3 and E4 the formula is 37 and 57 mm off, which anyone can see. For E1 (+1 %) the difference disappears in the measuring error, and for E2 (+3 %) it is a close call. That is exactly what makes the formula believable: after all, it was right twice.

Tips

  • PLA on PLA can slip, especially when E3 and E4 tip over their ends. Slipping shortens the rolled distance and makes the contradiction look smaller. Roll slowly with light pressure. If that does not help, put masking tape in the groove or roughen the rim of the disc a little.
  • Really do hand out the second sheet only when all the tables are full. The point of this version is that the measurements are fixed before anyone has an expectation.
  • There is a version with large paper templates and a tape measure in which the formula comes before the measurement. There the expectation can already affect the reading. With the rolling track, doubt tends to fall on the method (“it slipped”, “printed inaccurately”). If you show both orders, you can compare which explanation your participants tick in each case.
  • The rolling track is new and has not yet been tried out in use. Test it before you use it in front of an audience.

And what does this have to do with science?

In a well-known experiment, scientists with doctorates were asked to find the volume of spheres once by measuring and once with a formula. The formula was wrong on purpose. Almost all of them trusted the formula more than their own measurement, and some “improved” their measurements until they fitted better (Nissani and Hoefler-Nissani 1992).

With the rolling track the numbers are fixed before the formula appears. Even so, many people look for the error in themselves or in the tool first. But a formula is only as good as its justification, and a justification can sound convincing and still be wrong. When measurement and calculation do not agree, it pays to wonder, because that is exactly where science begins.
