The Hindu Thread

A thread is broken into little pieces, rolled into a ball and is whole again at the end. With a story from a London jazz cellar.

What this is about

Knowledge has never been as easy to get as it is today, and yet distrust of science keeps growing. More facts alone do little about it. The legal scholar Dan Kahan showed that people with more scientific knowledge are often even further apart on contested issues: they use what they know to defend the views of their own group.

What is missing is wonder. We feel wonder when the world turns out differently from what we expected. The brain predicted something, and something else happened. In that moment it does not defend itself, it learns. The philosopher Helen De Cruz therefore describes wonder as a feeling that tells us: your picture of the world needs revising.

Magic creates this moment reliably and with little effort. Anyone who sees a trick immediately asks: how can that be? Every science begins with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment along the triad of playing, wondering, understanding: try something out, be surprised, make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. The idea behind it is that wonder helps us learn better, opens our thinking and makes us less likely to mistake a mere guess for a fact. More under Background and in the book “Wissenschaft und Zauberkunst” (Science and Magic).

What your audience sees

You take an old thread card out of your breast pocket, show your hands empty and unwind a long piece of thread. While you tell a story, you break the thread into a dozen or so pieces. You keep one and roll the rest into a little ball. A touch of saliva, a firm squeeze. Then you slowly pull your hands apart, and between them hangs a single thread again, as long as your outstretched arms.

What you need

  • The thread card from the 3D printer, labelled like an old thread card (“tacking thread, pure cotton”). It has a pocket on the back, and the slits at the ends hold the thread. It lives in the compact box.
  • Thread. Use thin yarn rather than smooth sewing thread. It must break easily, and people should hear it break. A slightly off-white with a bit of texture is easier to see from a distance than pure white, which glares in a spotlight. After breaking, the ends should curl a little so that everyone sees separate pieces. Avoid synthetic fibre: it breaks badly and cuts into your fingers before you notice.
  • The right length. Stretch your arms as wide as you can and add a good hand’s width. That is how far you pull your hands apart at the end.
What is missing here: How the thread goes onto the card and what sits in the pocket is not on this page. Lewis Ganson describes the basic method in his booklet on the Hindu thread. The handling the card is built for belongs to Nick Lewin’s “Ultimate Gypsy Yarn”. If you want to perform it, buy his instructions.

The story

Nick Lewin tells a story from London with the thread. It happened to him: at eleven he saw the magician Billy McComb tear the thread at Conway Hall, and McComb later became his mentor. On stage Lewin usually turns it into a jazz cellar in Soho. Here it is in my own words, with his permission. Square brackets say what the hands are doing.

A magician once told me about a night in London. It was summer, but foggy and cold, the way only London manages. [card out of the breast pocket, show hands, unwind the thread]

He took the Underground to the West End, to Soho. A square mile of little bars, clubs and coffee houses, not exactly the smartest part of town. His favourite place was a jazz cellar. Two steps down, he said, and not only on the stairs.

That night a man jumped up on the stage. Nobody knew why. He had nothing with him, just two empty hands and a piece of thread. He stood in the middle and began to break the thread into little pieces. [break]

The band did not understand what he was doing. One after another they packed up. In the end only the saxophone player was left, still playing. And the man kept breaking, until he had maybe thirteen or fourteen pieces. [one piece drops, look after it] Thirteen.

Then he took out a single piece. The rest he rolled into a ball. [roll] A tiny bit of saliva. A firm squeeze. [squeeze, take your time]

And the thread stayed hanging in the air. Just like that. [hold up one end, show it to the side] He had made us a spitball, for our entertainment.

The audience was not particularly impressed. A bit like you right now. Until he did this. [slowly pull the hands apart]

Lewin himself advises finding your own story. When he learned that McComb had died, he told the true story that same evening instead of the jazz cellar. That, he says, is where he found the feeling the trick had been missing. The thread fits wherever something tears and becomes whole again: the thread of life, losing the thread, a quarrel, a reconciliation. In the end the audience takes home you more than the trick.

Performance tips

These come from Lewin’s explanation. He performs the trick in theatres with 3,000 seats, but they help in a classroom too.

  • A thread is tiny. Wear something dark, a black shirt or a jacket. In a white shirt, break the thread in front of the jacket and turn a little to the side.
  • Break close to the microphone so every break can be heard. Give each break a small movement of the shoulders. The back row cannot see the thread, but it sees the shoulders and knows something is being torn.
  • Make the pieces different lengths. Pieces of equal length look suspiciously tidy.
  • Look at the thread the whole time. Where you look, the audience looks.
  • Keep your hands open and your fingers spread.
  • Feel free to drop a piece. It gets a laugh and shows that the pieces really are loose.
  • Make a big thing of the saliva and the squeeze. And do not throw the ending away: pull your hands apart slowly and give the thread time to unfold. That is the moment everyone is waiting for.

And what does this have to do with science?

The audience does not see everything that happens in front of its eyes. It mostly sees what its attention is steered towards, and attention often follows the performer’s gaze. Gustav Kuhn and Michael Land measured this in 2006 with a different trick: in the vanishing ball illusion many spectators saw a ball fly upwards that was never thrown, especially when the magician looked after it. Lewin’s advice to keep your eyes on the thread is the same observation from the stage.

Afterwards it is worth asking the class: what did you see, and what did you only conclude? Anyone who says “he tore the thread” heard breaks and saw pieces. Nobody saw that the thread that is whole at the end is the same one. That is an inference that feels like an observation. Science makes the same mistake sometimes.
