The Motor in Your Hand

The homopolar motor is said to be the simplest electric motor in the world. A battery, a screw, a magnet and a piece of wire, and within seconds something spins that should not be a motor at all.

What this is about

Knowledge has never been as easy to get as it is today, and yet distrust of science keeps growing. More facts alone do little about it. The legal scholar Dan Kahan showed that people with more scientific knowledge are often even further apart on contested issues: they use what they know to defend the views of their own group.

What is missing is wonder. We feel wonder when the world turns out differently from what we expected. The brain predicted something, and something else happened. In that moment it does not defend itself, it learns. The philosopher Helen De Cruz therefore describes wonder as a feeling that tells us: your picture of the world needs revising.

Magic creates this moment reliably and with little effort. Anyone who sees a trick immediately asks: how can that be? Every science begins with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment along the triad of playing, wondering, understanding: try something out, be surprised, make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. The idea behind it is that wonder helps us learn better, opens our thinking and makes us less likely to mistake a mere guess for a fact. More under Background and in the book “Wissenschaft und Zauberkunst” (Science and Magic).

Before you start: This time there is no secret to keep. This is a real physics experiment, and you may show and explain everything. It still looks like magic. Read the “Safety” section first: the wire short-circuits the battery.

What your audience experiences

You take a small sleeve holding a battery, a screw with a magnet on its head and a piece of copper wire. The screw hangs by its tip from the underside of the battery. Then you hold the wire to the top of the battery and its other end against the side of the magnet. At once the screw and the magnet start to spin fast. No coil, no switch, no gears.

What you need

  • The printed sleeve for one AAA battery. The battery stands on a floor and sticks out 2.5 mm above the rim so the wire can reach the top terminal. The floor has a contact hole (Ø 4.5 mm, countersunk from below) through which the screw tip reaches the bottom terminal.
  • An AAA battery.
  • A 10 × 10 mm cylinder magnet made of neodymium. It needs to be strong enough.
  • A small steel screw, for example a wood or drywall screw with an 8 mm head. It has to be magnetic, so not brass.
  • About 15 cm of copper wire. It must not be magnetic, not even at the ends.

How the sleeve is built

The battery sits in the middle. On one side is the storage for screw and magnet: at the top there is a cradle that the magnet snaps into. It is so close to the battery that the magnet pulls itself towards the steel can. Below it a groove for the screw head and shank runs downwards; it is open at the bottom, so longer screws may stick out. On the other side there is a winding rail with two hooks. You wind the wire lengthwise around both hooks. One turn is about 96 mm, so your 15 cm make a good one and a half turns.

Simplified sketch; storage and winding rail are left out.

Preparation

Push the battery into the sleeve flat end first, so the pointed terminal sticks out at the top. Put the magnet on the head of the screw. For transport the magnet snaps into the cradle, the screw head and shank lie below it in the groove, and the wire is wound around the winding rail.

Performance

  1. Unpack. Unwind the wire from the rail. Pull the screw and magnet out of the storage; the magnet stays on the screw head.
  2. Show. Lay the parts out in front of your audience: a battery, a screw, a magnet, a piece of wire. Ask whether you could build a motor out of them.
  3. Attach the rotor. Hold the sleeve upright and guide the screw tip into the contact hole from below. It sticks magnetically to the bottom terminal of the battery, with the magnet hanging underneath.
  4. Close the circuit. With the index finger of the hand holding the sleeve, press one end of the wire onto the top terminal. With thumb and index finger of your other hand, hold the other end gently against the side of the magnet.
  5. It spins. The screw and magnet start rotating fast. Let the wire only just brush the magnet; the pressure lets you control the contact.
  6. Ask questions. Take the wire away and the motor runs down. Then ask what will happen if you turn the battery round, and have people predict before you try it.
  7. Pack away. Remove the wire, pull off the rotor, snap the magnet into the cradle, wind the wire around the rail.

Safety

  • Short circuit: wire, magnet and screw connect the two battery terminals directly. A very large current flows. Keep contact for a few seconds only and never leave the wire in place.
  • Heat: the wire, the contact points and the battery can get hot. Hold the wire away from the terminal, and take a break when something gets warm.
  • Magnet: neodymium magnets are very strong. Keep them away from watches, bank cards and other magnetic cards. Two magnets snapping together can pinch fingers or chip. Small children must not get hold of magnets they could swallow.
  • Storage: make sure that no wire in the sleeve or the case touches the top terminal and the screw or magnet at the same time. If you will not use the sleeve for a long time, take the battery out.

Why it works

The current flows from the battery through the wire into the magnet and back through the screw to the battery. On its way it has to cross the magnetic field of the cylinder magnet. A current flowing across a magnetic field feels the Lorentz force, at right angles to the current and to the field lines. You find its direction with the right-hand rule. This force comes with an equal and opposite reaction force, and that one acts on the magnet. It produces a torque.

Because the cylinder magnet is round, the whole arrangement looks exactly the same after every turn. So the force stays, and the first push becomes a continuous rotation. It is fast for three reasons: the short circuit delivers a large current, the neodymium magnet has a strong field, and friction is low. The screw has itself become magnetic and hangs by its tip from the terminal like a pivot bearing, and the wire only lightly touches the magnet.

If you turn the battery round, the current reverses and the motor spins the other way.

Tips

  • If nothing turns: are the wire ends bare? Is the screw made of steel? Is the battery fresh? Does the wire touch the side of the magnet and not its flat face?
  • Pressing too hard acts as a brake. The motor runs best when the wire just brushes the magnet.
  • If after printing the magnet sits too tight or too loose in the cradle, or the battery wobbles, you can adjust this in the print file.

And what does this have to do with science?

This motor lacks everything you expect from a motor: the coil that creates a second magnetic field, and the commutator that reverses the current at the right moment. People often only believe it works once they have seen it or tried it themselves. Yet in principle it is the same motor with which Michael Faraday first turned electric current into continuous motion in 1821.

Unlike a magic trick, the solution here is not “you were fooled” but “nature really is like this”. Wonder does not vanish with the explanation; it leads you to it. And one question remains that generations of physicists have wrestled with: does the magnetic field rotate with the magnet or not? And if it did rotate, could the magnet turn at all?
