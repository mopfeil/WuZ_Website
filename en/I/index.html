The reversible box

The inverter with its ball box. One ball alone runs to the box, two balls together keep their distance. And your spectator cannot make it work with the same box.

What this is about

Knowledge has never been as easy to get as it is today, and yet distrust of science keeps growing. More facts alone do little about it. The legal scholar Dan Kahan showed that people with more scientific knowledge are often even further apart on contested issues: they use what they know to defend the views of their own group.

What is missing is wonder. We feel wonder when the world turns out differently from what we expected. The brain predicted something, and something else happened. In that moment it does not defend itself, it learns. The philosopher Helen De Cruz therefore describes wonder as a feeling that tells us: your picture of the world needs revising.

Magic creates this moment reliably and with little effort. Anyone who sees a trick immediately asks: how can that be? Every science begins with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment along the triad of playing, wondering, understanding: try something out, be surprised, make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. The idea behind it is that wonder helps us learn better, opens our thinking and makes us less likely to mistake a mere guess for a fact. More under Background and in the book “Wissenschaft und Zauberkunst” (Science and Magic).

Before you read on: Magicians do not reveal secrets. This page is for you because you want to perform the trick yourself. The secret lies in the physics of magnets and in a single millimetre. Keep it to yourself, and try everything out before you show it.

What your audience sees

A flat, round box lies on the table. On top sits a small screw-top container holding two tiny ball magnets. You tell a story about two friends. One of them likes going to a place the other does not like going to at all.

You let one ball roll across the table towards the box. It is pulled in and sticks to the box: alone, he goes there. Then you put one ball on top of the other and push the little tower closer. A few millimetres from the box it stops, as if there were an invisible line. Together they will not go. If you slide the box across the table, the tower follows it at a fixed distance.

And if one of them is already there when the other arrives? Then both fly off.

What you need

  • The reversible box (InverterMagnet V9): base and lid, together 48.5 mm across and 9.1 mm high. Both parts have the same knurling on the outside, and the seam sits exactly at half height. Both outer faces carry the same QR code in the same place, leading to these instructions, so the box looks the same after turning it over.
  • Seven neodymium magnets, axially magnetised: one disc Ø 20 × 6 mm for the centre and six cylinders Ø 6 × 6 mm for the ring (pitch radius 15.5 mm). The six ring magnets face up with the opposite pole to the centre magnet.
  • Two ball magnets, 3 mm in diameter.
  • The ball box with its cap, a small screw-top container for storing the balls. It sits in a shallow recess and fits on either side of the box.
  • A smooth table without metal under the top. Iron in the table changes the magnetic field.

How the prop works

A large magnet sits in the centre of the box, with six small ones in a circle around it, poled the opposite way. This arrangement is called an inverter or tractor magnet. Why it pulls in one ball but keeps a tower of two balls at a distance is explained below under “What is behind it”.

All seven magnets sit in the base. Below them is a 1.0 mm floor, above them the 2.0 mm lid plate. From the outside you cannot tell; both sides look the same. With the thin side (floor) on the table, the magnets are only 1.0 mm above the table top, almost level with the balls. With the thick side (lid) down, they are 2.0 mm up. That one millimetre decides whether the tower reliably keeps its distance.

Preparation

Put the box on the table with the thin side down. Remember which side that is by some small feature, such as a scratch on the rim or the feel of the knurling. Place the ball box in the recess on top, put the two balls in and screw on the cap.

Try out beforehand from how far away a single ball is reliably pulled in, and how close you can push the tower before it stops.

Performance

  1. Open. Unscrew the cap, push the two balls out through the slot and put the ball box aside.
  2. The story. Tell the story of the two friends. One likes going to a place the other does not like going to. Where that is, you can make up yourself.
  3. Alone. Place one ball a few centimetres from the box and let it roll in. It is pulled in and sticks. “Alone, he goes.” Take it off again.
  4. Together. Put the second ball on top of the first so that they form a small tower. Push it slowly towards the box. It stops at a fixed distance. “Together, they don’t.” Slide the box a little across the table; the tower follows.
  5. The latecomer. Separate the balls. Let one roll to the box again and the second one after it. As soon as the second reaches the first, both fly off. “And if one is already there when the other comes, they both leave.”
  6. The spectator tries. Hand over the box for a look and, casually, put it back with the thick side down. Now the tower often does not keep its distance. Take the box back, turn it over as you do, and it works for you again.

What is behind it

Attraction and repulsion at once. The large centre magnet is stronger than the small ring magnets, so from a distance its attraction wins. As a magnet comes closer, the ring magnets are nearer to it than the centre, and their repulsion grows faster. In between there is a distance at which the two forces cancel. A magnet stops there, and if it drifts a little, the box pushes or pulls it back. The principle is sold as a kit under the name “Inverter Magnet” and was studied in detail by Michael Adams at the University of Luxembourg.

Why only on the table. Fixed magnets alone cannot hold another magnet stably in free space. This follows from a theorem Samuel Earnshaw proved in 1842: there is always some direction in which it can escape. With a tractor magnet, the table takes away the escape up and down. Only the directions in the plane remain, and there the distance can be stable. That is why the trick only works while the balls lie on the table top.

Why one ball goes in alone. A single ball can turn freely. It always turns its poles so that it is attracted, no matter whether the centre or the ring is closer. A freely turning magnet therefore always moves to where the field is strongest, and that is at the box. There is no line for it.

Why two keep their distance. Two balls on top of each other form a tower standing on the table. To flip its poles it would have to tip over. So it keeps its orientation, like the test magnet in the commercial kit, which is also made of two stacked magnets. That is why the tower feels both the pull of the centre and the push of the ring, and stops at the line.

Why the latecomer sends both away. One possible explanation: the first ball has turned itself to suit the box. When the second rolls up, the two snap together and suddenly form a tower with a fixed orientation. But that tower now stands much closer to the box than the line, where the ring’s repulsion wins, and it is pushed out.

Why the thick side spoils it. With the lid side down, the magnets sit one millimetre higher above the table top. The balls then feel a weaker and differently shaped field, and the line no longer holds the tower reliably. Magnetic forces fall off very quickly with distance, so that one millimetre is enough.

Tips

  • Test every newly printed box and every new set of balls. Other magnets or another printer can shift the line.
  • Let the tower roll in slowly or push it with a fingertip. A quick shove carries it over the line, and it sticks to the box after all.
  • Never let the spectator try both sides one after the other. They would compare, and the secret would be out.
  • Ball magnets do not belong in children’s hands. Swallowing two of them is life-threatening. Always put the balls back in the ball box after the performance.
  • Neodymium magnets are brittle. Do not let the balls snap hard against each other or against the box, or they will chip.
  • The magnets in the base sit in pockets 0.1 mm deeper than the magnets; the lid presses them down when screwed on. If the thread is too tight, a larger clearance can be set in the file.

And what does this have to do with science?

Your spectator did the same experiment with the same material, and yet the result was different. The difference lay in a condition nobody measured, because the box looks the same from both sides. This is how replications fail in research too: a detail that was taken for granted in the first lab is missing from the methods section. If you cannot reproduce a result, look for such hidden conditions first, before declaring one of the two experiments wrong.

And the box shows something else: the table seems to be just a surface to work on. In fact it is what makes the fixed distance possible at all. Boundary conditions you take for granted are part of the experiment.
