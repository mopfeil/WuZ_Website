The reversible box

The inverter with its ball box. A magnet experiment that works for you and fails for your spectator, although they use the same box and the same balls.

What this is about

A magic trick bursts a firm expectation. At exactly that moment we feel wonder, and wonder makes us curious and open: we want to know how it can be, and we are ready to correct our picture of the world. Every science starts with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment. First play and wonder, then make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. More under Background.

Before you read on: magicians do not give away secrets. This page is for you because you want to perform the experiment yourself. The secret of the reversible box is a single millimetre. Keep it to yourself, and try everything out before you show it.

What your audience sees

A flat, round box lies on the table. In the middle of its top sits a tiny screw-top box holding two small ball magnets. You unscrew the little box, push the balls out, and your magnet experiment works.

Then your spectator gets the box. It looks the same from above and below, and the little box fits on either side. They do exactly what you did, with the same balls. For them it does not work.

What you need

  • The reversible box (InverterMagnet V9): base and lid, together 48.5 mm across and 9.1 mm high. Both parts have the same knurling on the outside, and the seam sits exactly at half height. Both outer faces carry the same QR code in the same place, leading to these instructions, so the box looks the same after turning it over.
  • Seven neodymium magnets, axially magnetised: one disc Ø 20 × 6 mm for the centre and six cylinders Ø 6 × 6 mm for the ring (pitch circle radius 15.5 mm).
  • The ball box with its cap: a small screw-top box with an 8 mm compartment and a 4 mm slot in the side. The cap is 17 mm across.
  • Two ball magnets, 3 mm in diameter.

How the prop works

All seven magnets sit in the base, their top faces level. Below them is a floor of 1.0 mm; above them is the lid plate, 2.0 mm thick. You cannot see this from outside: lid and base meet exactly at the middle of the outer height, and the thread is hidden inside.

Both outer faces have a shallow recess in the middle (0.4 mm deep). The plug under the ball box fills it exactly, and the floor of the compartment is then flush with the outer face. So the balls lie 1.0 mm above the magnets on the base side and 2.0 mm on the lid side. That is the only difference.

The ball box is not glued on. The magnets hold it in the recess, and without it each side of the box is flat. That is why you can turn the box over and put the little box on the other side. Because the balls are pulled hard towards the box, you do not lift them out; you push them sideways through the slot with a fingertip. They roll out onto the box without a step.

Preparation

Try out beforehand, with your own balls, on which side your experiment reliably works and on which it does not. From the tests so far, the near side is the good one, that is the base side with 1.0 mm. Learn to recognise that side by some small feature, such as a scratch on the edge or the feel of the knurling.

Put the box down with the good side up, set the ball box into the recess, put both balls in and screw the cap on.

Performance

  1. Open. Unscrew the cap and push both balls through the slot onto the box.
  2. Perform. Show your experiment with the balls. It works, because you are on the good side.
  3. Collect. Push the balls back into the compartment and take the ball box off.
  4. Turn. Hand the box to your spectator and casually turn it over as you do. Put the ball box into the recess on the side that is now on top. For them it is the same box with the same little box.
  5. The spectator tries. They push the balls out and do everything as you did. For them the experiment fails.
  6. Back again. Take the box back, turn it over again as you do, and show it once more. It works for you again.

What is behind it

Magnetic forces fall off very quickly with distance. One extra millimetre of plastic between the balls and the magnets can decide whether an experiment works. That showed up while the box was being developed: with an earlier model the experiment worked better when the magnets were closer to the surface. Since then the floor has been only 1.0 mm below the magnets, and that led to the idea of a box with a near side and a far side.

The name comes from the first design, a frame for an “inverter magnet” (also called a “tractor magnet”): a centre disc with a ring of smaller magnets around it.

Tips

  • Test every newly printed box and every new set of balls. Different magnets or a different printer can shift the threshold.
  • Never let the spectator try both sides one after the other. They would compare, and the secret would be out.
  • Do not pick the balls up from above with your fingernails; push them. Otherwise they easily jump away and are hard to find.
  • Ball magnets do not belong in children’s hands. Swallowing two of them is life-threatening. Always put the balls back into the ball box after the performance.
  • The magnets in the base sit in pockets 0.1 mm deeper than the magnets; the lid presses them down as you screw it on. If the thread is too tight, the file has a setting for more play.

And what does this have to do with science?

Your spectator did the same experiment with the same material, and still got a different result. The difference lay in a condition nobody measured, because the box looks the same from both sides. This is also how replications fail in research: a detail that was taken for granted in the first lab is missing from the methods section. If you cannot reproduce a result, look for hidden conditions like this first, before deciding that one of the two experiments is wrong.
