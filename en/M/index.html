Circle or square?

The ambiguous cylinder after Kokichi Sugihara. A tube stands in front of a mirror. You see its rim as a circle, and in the mirror the same rim is a square.

What this is about

Knowledge has never been as easy to get as it is today, and yet distrust of science keeps growing. More facts alone do little about it. The legal scholar Dan Kahan showed that people with more scientific knowledge are often even further apart on contested issues: they use what they know to defend the views of their own group.

What is missing is wonder. We feel wonder when the world turns out differently from what we expected. The brain predicted something, and something else happened. In that moment it does not defend itself, it learns. The philosopher Helen De Cruz therefore describes wonder as a feeling that tells us: your picture of the world needs revising.

Magic creates this moment reliably and with little effort. Anyone who sees a trick immediately asks: how can that be? Every science begins with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment along the triad of playing, wondering, understanding: try something out, be surprised, make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. The idea behind it is that wonder helps us learn better, opens our thinking and makes us less likely to mistake a mere guess for a fact. More under Background and in the book “Wissenschaft und Zauberkunst” (Science and Magic).

Before you read on: this is an optical illusion, not a trick with a secret. You may explain at the end how it works. But let your audience wonder first, and do not show the tube from the side until everyone has seen the circle and the square.

What your audience sees

A short tube stands on the table in front of a small mirror. Seen from the front, the tube has a round rim, like a piece of drainpipe. The mirror shows the back of the same tube, and there the rim is angular: a square. Both can be seen at the same time.

Then you turn the tube half way round. Now the square is at the front and the circle is in the mirror.

What you need

  • The mirror tile, 100 × 100 × 3 mm. It is in the magic case.
  • The mirror stand. A plate of 120 × 95 mm with a bar and a slot at the back, into which the mirror tile is pushed upright. In front of it is a shallow recess with exactly the floor plan of the tube. It sets the tube square to the mirror. The stand is in the case as well, underneath the mirror tile.
  • A tube. There are two: circle at the front and square at the back, or circle at the front and diamond at the back. The tubes are up to 45 mm tall and there is no room for them in the case, so you have to print them separately and bring them along. Each tube has its own stand, because the recess only fits the floor plan of its own tube.
  • A phone with a camera, if you want to show the illusion to several people at once.

How the prop works

The top rim of the tube is not flat. It rises and falls: on the circle and square tube it lies between 17 and 45 mm above the base. From the side you see a high peak at the front; behind it the rim slopes down into a dip and rises a little again towards the back wall. The floor plan is not a circle either, but a rounded oblong of 40 × 56.6 mm.

The heights are calculated so that the rim looks exactly like a circle when seen at 45° from above and in front, and like a square when seen the same way from behind. The mirror stands upright behind the tube. It shows you the back, also at 45° from above. That puts both views into one picture.

The fact that the mirror swaps left and right does not matter: circle, square and diamond are symmetrical.

Preparation

Push the mirror tile into the slot of the stand. Put the tube in the recess with the circle side facing you. Place the stand on a table a little below eye level and try out where you need to stand: about a metre away or more, looking down at an angle of roughly 45°.

Performance

  1. Have a look. Invite someone to your spot. They look at the tube from the front and from above, ideally with one eye only, and say what shape the rim is. A circle.
  2. Look in the mirror. Without moving, they now look into the mirror. There the rim is a square.
  3. With the camera. For a larger group, hold the phone where the eye was, zoom in and pass the picture round or show it on a screen.
  4. Turn it round. Lift the tube out of the recess, turn it half way round about its upright axis and put it back. Now the square is at the front and the circle is in the mirror.
  5. Let them guess. Ask what the rim really looks like. Collect a few guesses.
  6. The reveal. Take the tube out and show it from the side. The rim is neither round nor angular, but curved up and down.

What lies behind it

The image on the retina is flat. From that image alone the eye cannot tell how far away each point of the rim is. The brain fills in the missing piece with an assumption: the rim of a tube is flat, and we are looking down on it at an angle. Under this assumption the curve you see from the front is a circle, and the curve in the mirror is a square. Most of the time the assumption is right, because almost every tube has a flat rim. Here it is wrong.

With both eyes or from close up the brain gets extra depth cues, and the illusion becomes weaker. That is why one eye, a greater distance or the camera helps.

Kokichi Sugihara, a Japanese mathematician, designed objects like this and won second place with one in the 2016 “Best Illusion of the Year” contest.

Tips

  • The viewing angle matters. The shapes are calculated for 45°. If you look flatter or steeper, the circle turns into an oval and the square into a trapezium. Try out beforehand where your audience should stand.
  • The shapes are made for a view from far away. From close up they are no longer quite right. Using the phone camera's zoom from a metre or more gives the cleanest result.
  • Do not let people walk round the table before you want to show the reveal. From the side it is obvious at once that the rim is not flat.
  • The circle and diamond tube works exactly the same way. The points of the diamond are trimmed very slightly so that the wall does not run out to nothing there.

And what does this have to do with science?

Perception is not a copy of the world but a hypothesis about it. The brain assumes that rims are flat and corners are right angles, because that is almost always true. This assumption makes us fast, but we are not aware of it, and so we do not check it. Only a second viewpoint, here the view from the side, shows that two apparently certain observations do not fit together. That is exactly how science works: make assumptions explicit, look at things from several directions and do not trust a single perspective, however convincing it seems.
