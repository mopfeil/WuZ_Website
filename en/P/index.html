The pendulum that follows your thoughts

A small pendulum on a thread, held still above the hand. Your spectator only thinks “left, right”, and after half a minute the pendulum swings exactly that way. They will swear they did not move.

What this is about

A magic trick bursts a firm expectation. At exactly that moment we feel wonder, and wonder makes us curious and open: we want to know how it can be, and we are ready to correct our picture of the world. Every science starts with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment. First play and wonder, then make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. More under Background.

Before you read on: There is no false bottom and no sleight of hand in this one. The pendulum really moves, driven by the hand of whoever holds it. Do not explain that beforehand. The experience only works if people have it themselves, and the explanation belongs at the end.

What your audience sees

Your spectator rests an elbow on the table, holds the thread between thumb and forefinger and lets the pendulum hang freely above their other, open hand. They keep the hand as still as they can and only imagine the pendulum swinging left and right. After 20 to 40 seconds it swings clearly for most people, and in the direction they imagined. When they think “forward and back”, the swing turns. When they think of a circle, the pendulum circles.

Then come two checks. With eyes closed the swing usually gets smaller, or its direction wanders. With the forearm and hand resting on the table, the effect usually collapses.

What you need

  • The pendulum from the magic case: a small plumb bob, 13 mm thick and a little over 23 mm long, made of two parts that screw together. A 5 × 5 mm cylinder magnet sits in the tip.
  • A thread of 40 to 50 cm. In a study of the Chevreul pendulum, 40 cm turned out to be the most successful length.
  • A table on which your spectator can rest an elbow.
  • If you want to measure: a phone with the Ideomotor pendulum app, and for a group the online version.

How the pendulum is built

The pendulum has the shape of a plumb bob. The lower part is a cone with a rounded tip. Just above the tip is a pocket for the magnet. The cap has a small hole on top for the thread and a threaded spigot underneath that screws into the lower part. Inside the spigot is a chamber for the knot. It is narrower than the magnet, so the end of the spigot also holds the magnet in its pocket.

The magnet sits in its pocket with a little play and is not glued. To assemble the pendulum or change the thread, simply unscrew it.

The magnet in the tip

You do not need the magnet for the basic routine. The pendulum works just as well without it, because the hand drives it. But the magnet allows an extra check: hold the pendulum above a piece of iron, and a real force pulls on it. That force still acts with eyes closed and the arm resting on the table. This lets your audience tell for themselves whether a force comes from outside or from their own hand.

Preparation

Thread the string from the top through the hole in the cap, tie a thick knot at the end of the spigot and pull it back into the chamber. Put the magnet into the pocket of the lower part and screw both parts together. Cut the thread to about 40 cm. For the magnet check, have a piece of iron ready, such as a large screw or nut.

Performance

  1. Posture. Your spectator rests an elbow on the table, holds the thread between thumb and forefinger and lets the pendulum hang freely above the open other hand.
  2. Only think. “Keep your hand as still as you can. Just imagine the pendulum swinging left and right.” Give them time. After 20 to 40 seconds it swings clearly for most people.
  3. Change direction. “Now forward and back.” The swing turns. Then: “Now in a circle.”
  4. First check. They close their eyes and carry on. Usually the swing gets much smaller, or the direction drifts.
  5. Second check. They rest the forearm and hand on the table so that only the fingertips are free. The effect usually collapses almost completely.
  6. The magnet check. Put the piece of iron on the table and have them hold the pendulum just above it, arm resting, eyes closed. If the magnet is strong enough, it is now pulled to the side anyway; try out beforehand how close you need to get. A force from outside survives both checks; the force from the hand does not.
  7. Collect first, then interpret. Do not explain right away. Ask: “What did your brain just do?” The observations from the checks are data. Collect them first, then interpret them together.
  8. Measure. Anyone who wants to know exactly can use the Ideomotor pendulum app. The phone lies flat on the hand, a disc swings on the screen, and a yellow crosshair shows, magnified 25 times, how much the hand really moved. You can watch yourself pushing.

Why it works

The effect is called ideomotor. Merely imagining a movement sends tiny impulses to the muscles, even when you do not intend to move. The movement is real and measurable. You just do not notice that you are making it.

That so little movement produces such a large swing is due to resonance. If the hand matches the pendulum’s natural rhythm, the swing builds up. With a 50 cm thread, one swing takes 1.42 seconds. If the hand moves just 2.2 millimetres in that rhythm, the result is a swing of about 60 millimetres. Around two millimetres is below the threshold at which we can still feel our own movement.

The chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul saw through this and described it in 1854. He placed a glass plate between the pendulum and its supposed source, and the pendulum kept swinging. He rested his hand on a support, and the pendulum went still. He blindfolded himself, and the effect disappeared. The eye supplies the feedback that keeps the hand unnoticed in the right rhythm. In 1852 William Carpenter named the whole thing the ideomotor principle.

Tips

  • Take your time. Anyone who gives up after ten seconds will miss the effect. A calm, quiet instruction helps more than pressure.
  • Knowing does not protect you. Even people who know the effect well get the pendulum swinging. That is a good moment for the debrief.
  • Screw the pendulum together by hand only. Before packing it away, wind the thread up loosely so that it does not tangle in the case.
  • With small children, be careful with the thread, and do not leave the unscrewed pendulum lying around: small magnets do not belong in children’s hands.

And what does this have to do with science?

The experience was clear: the hand stood still, the pendulum swung. And yet it was wrong. A better argument does not help against this kind of illusion; only a check that makes the unnoticed movement visible does. Chevreul did this with closed eyes and a support. In 1853 Michael Faraday built a small pointer device for table turning that showed whether the hands were pushing. As soon as people could see the pointer, the table stopped moving. The phone app is the same idea with an acceleration sensor.

The same pattern lies behind dowsing rods and the Ouija board. A good check separates what acts from outside from what we contribute ourselves. The magnet shows one side, the closed eyes the other.
