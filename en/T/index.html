The Trapdoor

A card with a small door. A few folds, pull gently apart, and the card is turned over.

What this is about

Knowledge has never been as easy to get as it is today, and yet distrust of science keeps growing. More facts alone do little about it. The legal scholar Dan Kahan showed that people with more scientific knowledge are often even further apart on contested issues: they use what they know to defend the views of their own group.

What is missing is wonder. We feel wonder when the world turns out differently from what we expected. The brain predicted something, and something else happened. In that moment it does not defend itself, it learns. The philosopher Helen De Cruz therefore describes wonder as a feeling that tells us: your picture of the world needs revising.

Magic creates this moment reliably and with little effort. Anyone who sees a trick immediately asks: how can that be? Every science begins with the same question. That is why each trick in the basic kit is also a small experiment along the triad of playing, wondering, understanding: try something out, be surprised, make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. The idea behind it is that wonder helps us learn better, opens our thinking and makes us less likely to mistake a mere guess for a fact. More under Background and in the book “Wissenschaft und Zauberkunst” (Science and Magic).

What your audience sees

You show a card. One side says “Zauberkunst” (magic), the other “Wissenschaft” (science), and in the middle there is a small door. You fold the card a few times, gently pull it apart, and in the end it is turned over.

What you need

  • The trapdoor card from the basic kit. Folded it is A8 size; for the trick you unfold it completely.

Step by step

The photos show the whole sequence. Fold slowly and do not press the creases too hard, so the card survives many performances.

  1. Fold in half

  2. and open again

  3. Fold the edges to the middle

  4. the other edges too

  5. Open again

  6. Hold the door

  7. Fold in the sides

  8. Top and bottom as well

  9. Fold once more

  10. Open the other way

  11. Gently pull apart

  12. Turned over, done

These instructions are brief. If you get stuck somewhere, just ask.

And what does this have to do with science?

Magicians use what they know about how the world works to make others believe in a magical reality. To do so they use illusions of perception and of thinking. If you learn to think like a magician, you find it easier to see where we deceive ourselves or others, on purpose or not. That makes it easier to check whether the picture we have of the world is an honest one.
