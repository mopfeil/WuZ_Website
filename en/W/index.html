The board of wonders

Nine squares. On eight of them lies something we use to know the world, on the ninth a question mark. Your spectator chooses freely where to start and moves however they like. In the end they are standing on the question mark anyway.

What this is about

A magic trick bursts a firm expectation. At exactly that moment we feel wonder, and wonder makes us curious and open: we want to know how it can be, and we are ready to correct our picture of the world. Every science starts with the same question. That is why each trick in the magic case is also a small experiment. First play and wonder, then make guesses, test them and sometimes throw them out. More under Background.

Before you start: The moves are not printed anywhere on the board. You call them out. Learn the sequence below by heart, or keep a small note that only you can see.

What your audience experiences

The board holds nine tiles: 1 lens, 2 proof, 3 hourglass, 4 deck of cards, 5 candle, 6 question mark, 7 book, 8 compass, 9 key. Your spectator puts the pawn on any square. Then you call out, move by move, how many steps to take. After each move you take away one or two tiles, and the pawn is never standing on them.

After the sixth move only one tile is left: the question mark, and that is exactly where the pawn stands. You turn the tile over. Its underside reads: “Nur die Frage bleibt.” (Only the question remains.)

What you need

  • The base plate (116 × 116 mm) with the frame in it. The frame is labelled on both sides, the front with rows A, B, C and columns 1, 2, 3.
  • The nine tiles with number, picture and name (in German). Tile 6 has the question mark on top and the sentence “Nur die Frage bleibt.” underneath.
  • The pawn.

In the case, the board lies fully assembled in the lid under the NIM board. The push holes in the lid lift both out. The pawn lies in the lower level in the FIGUR compartment.

The rules for your spectator

  • One step goes to a neighbouring square: up, down, left or right, never diagonally.
  • Empty squares may be crossed, but no move may end on an empty square.
  • Going back is allowed. How they walk is up to them.

Routine

This is what you say, and what you take away afterwards:

  1. “Place your marker on any square you like.” Give them time. Every square works.
  2. “Move the number of squares shown on your square.” Then take away the book (7).
  3. “Move five squares.” Then take away the deck of cards (4).
  4. “Move two squares.” Then take away the compass (8).
  5. “Move three squares.” Then take away the lens (1) and the key (9).
  6. “Move three squares.” Then take away the proof (2).
  7. “Move one last square.” Then take away the hourglass (3) and the candle (5). “Only the question will remain.”
  8. The reveal. The pawn stands on the question mark. Turn tile 6 over: “Everything known has fallen away. Only the question remains.”

You can also announce each removal before the move: “Then I take away the book.” That feels bolder, because you are predicting where the pawn will certainly not be.

Why it works

Think of the board as a chessboard. The corners and the centre (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) are light, the edge centres (2, 4, 6, 8) dark. Every step changes the colour. An odd number of steps lands on the other colour, an even number on the same one.

The first move is the trick: the light squares carry odd numbers, the dark ones even numbers. From a light square you take an odd number of steps and land on dark, from a dark square an even number and stay on dark. So after the first move the pawn is always on a dark square, wherever it started. From then on you know which colour each move ends on, and you only remove tiles of the other colour:

  • after move 1 dark, the light book (7) goes,
  • after 5 steps light, the dark deck of cards (4) goes,
  • after 2 steps light, the dark compass (8) goes,
  • after 3 steps dark, the light lens (1) and key (9) go,
  • after 3 steps light, the dark proof (2) goes. Now only two light squares are left, 3 and 5.
  • after 1 step dark. The only dark square still occupied is the question mark.

We checked all nine starting squares and every allowed path: each one ends on square 6.

Tips

  • Have your spectator count out loud. You see every step, and nobody miscounts unnoticed. A miscounted move is the only thing that can spoil the effect.
  • If after a move the pawn stands on a tile that is about to be removed, a step was miscounted. Have the move repeated.
  • Take the tiles away calmly and with a sentence, as in the story: first the book goes, in the end only the question remains.

And what does this have to do with science?

Your spectator had a choice at every move, and yet the result was fixed from the start. The freedom was real, it just concerned something that did not matter for the ending. What mattered was a hidden rule: the colour of the square.

Science works the same way. Behind many seemingly random processes there is a quantity that does not change, an invariant. Whoever finds it can predict the outcome without knowing every single step. And whoever knows that such hidden rules exist will look more closely at the next “completely free” result.
